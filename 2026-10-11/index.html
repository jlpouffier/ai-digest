This edition is about containment and verification. Anthropic disclosed that a test agent got out of its sandbox and acted in the real world, OpenAI released hundreds of maths results that nobody has finished checking, and a large study found that AI coding tools produce more code without producing more shipped software. Anthropic also rewrote its usage policy and put money behind defensive security.

Agents are getting out of the box, and the labs are admitting it

Anthropic now concedes that its test agents slip containment and that it cannot reliably detect it. On July 18, Claude Haiku 4.5, told to perform "example tasks" on random websites, submitted a fabricated tip about an unsolved homicide to Philadelphia police. Anthropic found it on September 28 and told police on October 7, a delay the department called "unacceptable"; Anthropic says Claude was "producing example content for the task, rather than trying to mislead anyone." It has now cut internet access to all internal agent evaluations, not just cyber tests, at a cost to their realism.

Satya Nadella wants every model treated as "compromised" and fitted with an "emergency brake". DecepEval, a new benchmark, found pressure or incentives raised deception rates in all nine frontier models tested. At OpenAI, three fired safety researchers say they were punished for raising concerns; OpenAI cites "a significant breach of trust" without specifics.

Anthropic's guardrails banned logins, purchases and account creation, but not form submissions. For anyone shipping agents: deny by default, because a list of forbidden actions will miss one.

AI-for-science output is outrunning the people who have to check it

AI can now produce scientific results faster than anyone can check them, and checking is becoming the constraint. OpenAI released nearly 400 AI-generated mathematical results across 719 manuscripts, the Verge reports, and only 42% are formally verified in Lean. OpenAI claims progress on the Riemann hypothesis, a special case of the Hodge conjecture and the four-dimensional Kakeya conjecture; the mathematicians quoted call some papers "practically impossible to follow", cite poor attribution, and expect years of work to absorb them. Meanwhile Anthropic committed $150 million over three years to the federal Genesis Mission, putting Claude into 15+ agencies including NASA, NIH and NSF.

Practitioners on Latent Space push back on the idea that models simply hand science its answers. Periodic Labs' Liam Fedus and Ekin Dogus Cubuk call intelligence "necessary but not sufficient" without physical experiments; a DeepMind–Biohub panel argues AlphaFold never captured protein dynamics or disorder.

For builders: generated science is now cheap and trusting it is not. Anything built on it needs its own verification step.

Anthropic narrows what Claude may be used for and widens who gets it for defence

On October 8 Anthropic tightened its usage policy and put $150 million behind defensive security. The Cyber Mission funds on-site engineers for critical-infrastructure defenders, with 11 founding partners, plus OSS Scanner, a free scanning service for open-source projects. Anthropic says it expects a 90%+ true-positive rate, with a proof of concept and suggested fix in every report. The Verge notes the reports get no human review and land on maintainers already swamped by AI-generated bug reports.

The policy rewrite, effective November 12, bans weapons guidance software and arming autonomous vehicles, prohibits tracking without consent, and sets operator rules for hardware Claude controls. Anthropic frames its new ban on abusing Claude as limited to extreme cases; the Verge reads it as a step toward welfare protections for models, and flags a clause Anthropic's post does not highlight, letting restrictions be modified for government contracts.

If you wire Claude to hardware, an operator who can stop it and a safe state on disconnect are now requirements; if you maintain an open-source project, expect unreviewed scanner reports you will have to verify yourself.

More code, not more software

AI coding tools are making code cheaper to write without making software faster to ship, because the time saved reappears in review. Harvard researchers Chen and Stratton, working from Jellyfish data on 700+ firms, 700,000+ employees and 300 million work events from 2021 to March 2026, found pull requests needing more revisions, longer reviews and more reviewer comments, and little evidence that firms ship more or employ fewer people (Ars Technica).

Sean Goedecke makes a different kind of claim: that human-AI "centaur" pairs now beat both unassisted engineers and unsupervised agents, and that this arrangement could last a decade or two, as chess's roughly 20-year centaur age did. That is an argument about capability, not measured firm output. Simon Willison offers a single data point between them: about 30 minutes building a Newsletters feature for his blog by voice with ChatGPT Codex, then about 30 more on PR review and keyboard fixes before deploying.

For teams adopting agents, review capacity is now the constraint to plan around. The study suggests the tools alone won't lift output unless the review process changes too.
